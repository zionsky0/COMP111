What is AI?
▶The goal of work in AI is to build machines that perform tasks normally
requiring human intelligence. (Nilsson 1971)▶AI is everything we can’t do with today’s computers. (Possibly McCarthy,
1980s)
▶My definition of AI is any algorithm that is new in computer science.
Once the algorithm becomes accepted then it’s not AI, it’s just a boring
algorithm. At one time windows, mouse, menus, scroll bars etc. were
considered an AI technique. (Possibly Keane, 1990s)
▶A branch of computer science dealing with the simulation of intelligent
behavior in computers. The capability of a machine to imitate intelligent
human behavior. (Merriam Webster Dictionary)
The AI effect
“As soon as it works, no one calls it AI any more.” Optical character
recognition, spam filters, route planning and machine translation were all
flagship AI problems; today they are simply computer programs.
Vote
Which of these is AI? (a) a chess engine searching millions of positions; (b) a
spam filter trained on a million emails; (c) a thermostat; (d) a sat-nav running
Dijkstra; (e) ChatGPT writing a poem.

AI is both science and engineering
▶the science of understanding intelligent entities: developing
theories/models which attempt to explain and predict the
nature of such entities.▶the engineering of intelligent entities.
Compare aeronautics: understanding how birds fly is a different
project from building an aircraft . Aircraft do not flap their wings
— but they do obey aerodynamics.

Basic Terminology
▶AGI (artificial general intelligence, or strong AI): artificial
system that can accomplish any intellectual task that humans
can perform. Often sentience or consciousness are also
required in definitions of AGI.▶Narrow (or weak) AI: artificial system that is able to solve a
small number of specific intellectual problems. Does not
experience consciousness.
Before ChatGPT, other large language models (LLMs), and more
generally generative AI, came up in 2022/23, almost all AI
researchers and practitioners would have described their goal as
narrow AI. That has changed a bit: some argue that generative AI
is a significant step towards AGI, while others regard current
systems as very broad narrow AI.

Terminology: AI vs. ML vs. deep learning vs. generative AI
These terms are used interchangeably in the media. They are not synonyms.
Artificial Intelligence
search, logic planning
Machine Learning
Deep Learning
Generative
AI▶Machine learning: behaviour is learned from data rather than programmed.
▶Deep learning: ML with many-layered neural networks; features are learned, not
hand-crafted.
▶Generative AI: models that generate content (text, images, code) from a prompt.
▶Not all AI is ML: a chess engine’s search, or a Prolog program, learns nothing.

Thinking Humanly vs Acting Humanly
▶Cognitive science: to understand and build intelligent
machines we should simulate human thinking.
▶Computer science (often): to build intelligent machines
simulate the intelligent behavior of humans. It is not
necessary nor desirable that an intelligent machine processes
information in the same way as humans (think humanly); the
aim is to create machines that are able to behave (e.g., take
part in conversations) in the same way as humans.
The emphasis on simulating behavior rather than internal processes
has consequences for the question: when can we count a machine
as being intelligent ?“Can machines think?” −→“Can machines behave intelligently?”

Criticisms of the Turing Test
▶It rewards deception, not intelligence: to pass, a machine
must pretend to be bad at arithmetic.▶It is anthropocentric: aeroplanes are not tested by whether
they fool ornithologists.
▶It is not a useful engineering benchmark: not diagnostic, not
decomposable, and heavily dependent on the interrogator’s
skill.
▶The ELIZA effect (Weizenbaum 1966): people attribute
understanding to very shallow systems. ELIZA was a few
pages of pattern-matching rules, and users confided in it.
▶Searle’s Chinese Room (1980): a system might produce
perfect answers by manipulating symbols with no
understanding.
Modern practice: replace the Turing test by task-specific
benchmarks — and remember Goodhart’s law (“when a measure
becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”).

Thinking and Acting Rationally
▶Investigating how we actually think and act is one route to AI.
But how about how we should think and act?▶Use techniques from logic and probability theory to create
machines that reason correctly. The result is idealised
reasoning.
▶Acting rationally = acting to achieve one’s goals, given one’s
beliefs. An agent perceives an environment and acts on it; a
rational agent chooses actions expected to maximise its
performance measure. This is the framework for the rest of
the module.
▶Also use techniques from economics/game theory to create
machines that act rationally.
▶Rationality is not omniscience: doing the best you can with
the information and computation available is called bounded
rationality.
▶Problem of current generative AI: it often does not reason
correctly.

Optimism regarding AGI (1956–70)
Promising performance of early AI systems on simple examples
(integration problems, algebra problems, moving blocks) led to
great expectations. Simon (1957):
It is not my aim to surprise or shock you — but the simplest
way I can summarize is to say that there are now in the world
machines that can think, that can learn and that can create.
Moreover, their ability to do these things is going to increase
rapidly until — in a visible future — the range of problems
they can handle will be coextensive with the range to which the
human mind has been applied.Also from this era: Rosenblatt’s perceptron (1958), an early
trainable neural network, with huge press attention. Minsky &
Papert (1969) then showed that a single-layer perceptron cannot
even learn XOR — and neural network funding dried up for fifteen
years.

Combinatorial Explosion (Chess)
In principle, it is easy to write a program playing chess by
computing all possible sequences of moves (say, up to 40 moves)
and then selecting an optimal move. In chess, however, there are
approximately
▶400 different positions after one move apiece;
▶72,084 different positions after two moves apiece;
▶9×106positions after three moves apiece;
▶288×109different possible positions after four moves apiece;
▶and so on — the whole game tree is roughly 10120(the
“Shannon number”).For comparison: there are about 1080atoms in the observable
universe.
In practice, it simply is not possible (and never will be possible) to
compute all relevant sequences of moves! Growth is exponential in
the depth: bdfor branching factor b.

Combinatorial Explosion (Rubik’s Cube)
For a Rubik’s Cube there are
▶43,252,003,274,489,856,000 combinations ( ≈4.3×1019);
▶up to 481,229,803,398,374,426,442,198,455,156,736 brute-force
solution attempts.
(For comparison: the 2019 Summit machine at Oak Ridge National Lab
executes roughly 1018instructions per second.)Insight: the fact that a program can find a solution in principle does not
mean it can find it in practice . And yet every cube can be solved in at most
20 moves (“God’s number”, proved 2010): good heuristics explore a
vanishing fraction of the space. (Topic of the next section.)
The Lighthill Report (1973) for the British Research Council concluded that
AI researchers had failed to address the combinatorial explosion, and was
pessimistic about general robotics and natural language processing. It
formed the basis for the UK government’s decision to essentially end
support for AI research: the first AI winter.

Expert Systems (Narrow AI)
▶General purpose, brute force techniques don’t work, so use knowledge
rich solutions. The early 1980s saw the emergence of expert systems:
systems exploiting knowledge about tightly focused domains to solve
problems normally considered the domain of experts. Architecture: a
knowledge base of IF–THEN rules elicited from experts, plus an
inference engine.▶Examples (combining basic logic and probability based reasoning):
▶MYCIN : diagnose infections, recommend antibiotics. Matched
specialists in trials — never deployed.
▶XCON : configuring DEC VAX hardware; reportedly saved DEC millions
per year.
▶PROSPECTOR : aid geologists in mineral exploration, finding sites for
drilling.
▶1990s: most companies set up to commercialise expert systems did not
survive. Problems:
▶the knowledge elicitation bottleneck: rules must be written by hand, and
experts cannot articulate what they know;
▶brittleness: no common sense; one step outside the domain and answers
are nonsense;
▶lack of trust in the recommendations given.
▶Result: the second AI winter ( ≈1987–93). The field then turned
quietly from hand-coded rules to learning from data and to probability
(Bayesian networks, statistical NLP).

IBM Watson wins Jeopardy! (2011)
▶Jeopardy! is an American television quiz show: contestants are

pressure.▶Example:
▶Clue: In the 2004 opening ceremonies of the Olympic Games a sole

superhuman at Go, chess and shogi

AlphaFold: predicting 3D protein structures (2020)
▶Proteins underpin the biological processes of all living beings.

▶There are hundreds of millions of known proteins. Each has a
unique 3D shape that determines how it works and what it
does.
▶Predicting that 3D shape from the sequence of amino acids
had been an unsolved challenge for over 50 years; determining
one structure experimentally could take years.
▶Essentially solved by Google DeepMind in 2020; the public

Chemistry 2024 for Demis Hassabis and John Jumper.

deep neural networks — plus decades of curated public data.The pattern: a hard scientific problem + a large, clean dataset + deep learning.

Self-driving cars and RoboCup
▶Self-driving car: a vehicle sensing its environment and navigating
without human input. 1995: CMU’s NavLab 5 drove 2,849 miles,
98.2% autonomously — but the last 1.8% is where the difficulty lives.▶2004 DARPA Grand Challenge: none of the vehicles finished the
150-mile desert route (best: 7.32 miles). One year later, five vehicles
completed it.
▶Today driverless taxis operate commercially in several cities, but
progress has been far slower than predicted, and trust, liability and
regulation remain the hard part. Progress came from better sensors and
machine learning for perception, mapping and localisation.
▶RoboCup (since 1997): by mid-century, a team of autonomous
humanoid robots shall beat the human World Cup winners under FIFA
rules. See https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RoboCup .
▶Why is football harder than chess? Moravec’s paradox: high-level
reasoning needs little computation; low-level perception and motion
need enormous amounts.

Deep learning to generative AI (2012–)
▶2012, AlexNet: a deep convolutional network halved the error rate on
the ImageNet challenge. Ingredients: a large labelled dataset, GPUs,
and depth. (2024 Nobel Prize in Physics: Hinton and Hopfield, for
foundational work on neural networks.)▶2017, transformers: “Attention Is All You Need” introduced the
architecture behind essentially every modern large model. Attention
lets the model weigh the relevance of every other token, and it
parallelises well on GPUs.
▶2018–20, pre-training: train once on huge amounts of unlabelled text
(predicting the next word), then adapt to many tasks — instead of
building a separate system per task.
▶2022, ChatGPT: a chatbot based on the GPT-3.5 large language
model (OpenAI); the fastest-adopted consumer software product in
history.
▶Leading families in 2026 include GPT (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic),
Gemini (Google DeepMind), Grok (xAI), and open-weight models such
as Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek. (This list will be out of date by the time you read
it — itself a data point.)
▶Scale: trained on trillions of words, with 1011–1012parameters, at a cost of tens to
hundreds of millions of dollars per training run. Scaling laws: quality improves
smoothly with data, parameters and compute.
▶Post-training matters as much as pre-training: instruction tuning and reinforcement
learning from human feedback (RLHF) turn a text predictor into an assistant.

How an LLM works — and how it fails
The capital of France is ?P(“Paris”) = 0 .92
P(“the”) = 0 .03
P(“Lyon”) = 0 .01
▶The model computes a probability distribution over the next token and
samples from it. Repeat. That is all it does at inference time.▶So it has no database of facts to look up and no notion of truth — it
has statistics. A hallucination (e.g. an invented but perfectly plausible
citation) is therefore expected behaviour, not a bug.
▶Mitigations: retrieval (give the model the actual documents), tool use
(calculator, search, theorem prover), and reasoning models that are
trained to think step by step before answering.
▶Agents chain these together: read a repository, edit code, run the tests,
iterate. Note that this reunites machine learning with the old traditions
of search and knowledge representation.

Major Challenge: Explainable AI
▶In many domains it is important to understand the decisions
and predictions of AI algorithms. Examples: clinical decision
support, recruitment, credit scoring, financial advice.▶Many AI algorithms (in particular deep learning) are naturally
opaque, and typically even experts cannot explain their
decisions or predictions.
▶Lots of current research into explaining such decisions: feature
attribution, counterfactuals (“you would have been approved
if. . . ”), and interpretability of the network’s internal structure.
▶Warning: an explanation that is plausible but not faithful is
worse than none.

▶AI has no single agreed definition, and the label moves as
techniques succeed (the AI effect).▶Four views: thinking/acting ×humanly/rationally. Modern AI
mostly builds agents that act rationally.
▶Two AI winters were caused by over-promising plus the
combinatorial explosion: what works on toy problems need not
scale.
▶Each milestone has a technical reason: Deep Blue (search +
hardware), Watson (NLP + knowledge extraction), AlphaGo
(search + deep RL), AlphaFold (deep learning + data), LLMs
(transformers + scale + post-training).
